# Chapter 11: Upsell Offers — The Rollover Upsell

## Core Idea
**Credit some or all of a customer's previous purchases toward your next offer** — including purchases they made at *other* businesses. Framing money they already spent as credit gets far more people to take the next thing.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Design Questions**: **Who** to upsell · **what** to upsell · **how** to roll the credit over.
- **The Four Rollover Situations (the "who")**:
  1. **Re-engage customers who left a while ago** (win-back)
  2. **Rescue upset customers** — a better alternative to a refund
  3. **Rescue *other people's* upset customers**
  4. **Upsell regular customers**
- **The "what"**: more of what they just got · something better · something new and different. *"To make money, roll their credit over to something more expensive."*
- **The "how"**: apply all or part of the credit **upfront**, or **spread it over time.** (A $600 first purchase becomes a $50/month rollover discount across 12 months.)
- **The 4× Pricing Rule**: Price the next offer **at least 4× the rollover credit.** Then even crediting the entire first purchase is at most a **25% discount** — so profit survives. *"Bigger discounts make you less profit per sale, but they get more sales."*
- **Urgency**: Make rollover upsells **one time only.** *"If you're spicy, make the moment you present the offer the time to take it. A once-in-a-customer-lifetime offer. They don't get to sleep on it."*

## Key Concepts
- **You don't have to credit the full amount.** Roll over whatever you think will incentivize the next purchase, and test for the sweet spot.
- **Do rollover upsells before refunding.** "This has saved me tons of customers and cash. If you did a bad job — hey, it happens. **Roll over for a do-over.** And if they want something different, roll their purchase toward that instead."
- **Previous customers are still customers.** Reach out to anyone 6+ months since last purchase, look at what they paid, decide how much to roll over, and offer it.
- **Competitor rescue as a lead source**: Scrape contact information from **negative product reviews** where available — "a hot new list of leads who need what you have." Bonus play: *create* a place for people to complain about products in your industry, then rollover-upsell all of them.

## Mental Models
- **Sunk money becomes a gift.** The customer already spent it; you're the one who gives it back to them as value.
- **A refund returns money and ends the relationship. A rollover returns value and extends it.**
- **Their loyalty to a competitor is often just a contract.** Credit what they still owe elsewhere and the switching cost disappears.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rolling over into something too cheap**: If the next offer isn't ~4× the credit, you're discounting yourself out of profit.
- **Leaving the offer open-ended**: Without urgency, a surprise credit becomes something to think about, and thinking kills it.
- **Refunding first, offering second**: Once the money is back, the relationship is over.

## Worked Example
**The win-back campaign that paid for one day of work.** Hormozi made **personalized videos for 200 past customers**, offering each **$4,000 of credit** to return. **About 20% took it.** *"One day of recording videos got us an extra $1,900,000 of annual revenue. Worth it."*

**Four scripts, one per situation:**

| Situation | Who | What | How | Script |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Chiropractor win-back** | Customers 6 months since last purchase | New plan | Upfront | *"Hi Mrs. Banks, I wanted to give you your money back — do you have a minute? I wanted to see how your back pain is going. Sorry to hear that. Well, I have some good news. As a way of saying thank you, I want to give you **$500 of your money back as credit** toward staying pain-free for good. Is that of any interest?"* |
| **Dentist rescue** | Your own upset customer | Teeth whitening package | Front-load $200 credit | Person paid $200 for a cleaning but doesn't think their teeth got whiter. Explain they need more to get more, then **credit the $200** toward a whitening package with multiple sessions, an at-home kit, and deep cleanings |
| **Software competitor rescue** | Competitor's upset customers | Service agreement | Roll over their cost to break the old agreement | *"Hi John, I saw your negative review on their product and it really upset me. To make it up to you, **I'll credit whatever payments you have left with them** to switch to ours. This way you don't lose a thing and you start getting the benefits now. Fair enough?"* |
| **Membership** | Current customers | 12-month membership | Spread the first purchase across the term | Someone buys a small block of service — immediately offer to apply the **entire amount** toward 12 months. A $600 first purchase becomes **$50/month off for 12 months** |

**The gift card play** — rollover used as an *attraction* offer:
1. Advertise gift cards at **90%+ off** — e.g. **$200 gift cards for $20**
2. **Limit two per customer** and specify **they can only be used on other people**
3. Customers buy them as gifts for friends — an excellent holiday offer
4. When they buy, **ask who they want it made out to and whether they'll make an introduction**
5. When the friend comes in, **roll the gift card over** into an offer priced so the card is worth ~20% of it (a $200 card against a $1,000+ offer)

*"People pay you to refer their friends. It's pretty great. Plus, you get some pocket change from unused gift cards."*

## Key Takeaways
1. Credit past spending — yours or a competitor's — toward a bigger next offer.
2. Four target groups: lapsed, upset-yours, upset-theirs, current.
3. Price the next offer at 4× the credit so a full rollover is only a 25% discount.
4. Always try a rollover before issuing a refund.
5. Make it one-time-only so surprise converts instead of stalling.
6. Negative reviews are a rollover prospect list.
7. Discounted gift cards turn customers into paid referrers.
