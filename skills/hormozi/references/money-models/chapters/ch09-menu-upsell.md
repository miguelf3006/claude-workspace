# Chapter 9: Upsell Offers — The Menu Upsell

## Core Idea
Tell customers what they **don't** need, then tell them what they **do** need, ask their preference between two options, and make paying frictionless. Never ask *whether* they want to buy.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Menu Upsell Tactics, in order**:
  1. **Unsell** — tell them what they don't need, so you can emphasize what they do. Cross items out in front of them.
  2. **Prescribe** — tell them what they *do* need, with detailed personalized instructions. Two required components: **explain how it integrates with what they already bought**, and **personalize in detail how to maximize its value.**
  3. **A/B upsell** — ask which they prefer, not whether they want it. *"When you give people the option to not buy, some don't buy. So I give the option to pick between buying two similar things."*
  4. **Card on file** — *"Do you want to just use the card on file?"* Instead of asking whether they want to pay, refer to a way they already have.
- **Why each works**: every tactic **removes the option of not buying** from the question being asked.
- **Anything can be made A/B**: quantity (one bottle or two) · start dates (tomorrow or Monday) · payment (cash or card) · flavours · time slots (morning or afternoon) · media (read or listen) · delivery speed · sizes · colours · materials · personnel (John or Sarah) · communication (call or text).
- **Add a nudge to every A/B**: *"This is my favourite" · "X is usually a safe bet" · "A lot of people love this" · "Tuesday sessions are a little smaller if you like that" · "Amy does great with high schoolers."* If you want to move one product faster, nudge that one more.
- **The Economist Play** (when you have two options and want people to buy both): Present three options, pricing option **C at the same price as the more expensive option B.**

## Key Concepts
- **Card on file lowers hidden costs**: picking which card, taking it out, being reminded of ugly past buying decisions, the hassle of buying in a rush. *"If you make it easy for people to buy, more people will. This took me 10 years to learn. May you get the same value in 10 minutes."* For first purchases, the version is: *"What card do you want to use?"*
- **Unselling low-margin items incentivizes high-margin upsells.**
- **Employees love unselling.** They often like helping customers game the system — so **let them, on purpose.** They have inside knowledge; allowing them to show customers how to extract the most value makes everyone win.
- **Sold out? Take payment and delay delivery.** Selling, ordering, and setting arrival expectations lets you offer far more selection without carrying inventory. *"You'd be surprised how well this works."*
- **The tier-shifting rule**: lots of customers buying small → bump them to medium. Lots buying medium → bump to large. Lots buying large → **raise all your prices.**
- **Upsell your guarantees, warranties, and insurance** rather than giving them away. An art studio used to replace damaged portraits free; Hormozi told them to ask for an extra 10%. **Now 30% of customers buy what used to be free. Pure profit.**

## Mental Models
- **Ask which, never whether.** The single most portable idea in the chapter.
- **Detail sells.** "Detailed and personalized instructions upsell more people than vague and general suggestions." He calls this **prescription upselling.**
- **Crossing things out builds the goodwill that pays for the ask.** He eventually kept products in stock *just to cross them out.*

## Anti-patterns
- **Leading with benefits and science**: Full shelves and cool science both failed. Nineteen consults, zero sales.
- **Yes/no questions at the point of purchase**: Every one of them is an invitation to decline.
- **Two options where one is strictly cheaper and comparable**: The Economist's original mistake — print sales collapsed.

## Worked Example
**How he learned it — three accidents.**

*December 2013.* Nineteen nutrition consults, nobody bought anything. On appointment twenty he got so nervous he forgot his script and just asked: *"You've got a protein shake for breakfast — do you like chocolate or vanilla?"* → *"Which one's your favourite?"* → *"Chocolate."* → *"Great, I'll take one of those."* Then: *"Do you want kiwi or strawberry lemonade pre-workout?"* Then, to close: he grabbed her membership contract that already had her card on it — *"You just want to use the card we have on file?"* → *"Yep, that's fine."* **He then sold the next 20 customers in a row and cleared the shelf.**

*August 2014.* A customer wouldn't stop asking how to take everything — when, how many, at work, at home, at the gym. To end it, he wrote step-by-step instructions on scratch paper. The next appointment had overheard and asked for the same thing, so he wrote the instructions **directly on the order form**, next to each item. Then, wanting to stay on schedule, he skipped the pitch entirely: *"I've got all your instructions here. Do you want to just use the card on file?"* → *"Yeah, that's fine."* **30-day profit skyrocketed.**

*November 2016.* Selling supplements on the road — 40–50 people a day, two every 30 minutes, 12 hours straight — he ran out of product. So he told the next customer honestly: *"Sorry, I just ran out, but you can get something close at the shop down the street for about $20 less. It's not as good, but it'll do okay for the first month."* She was grateful. He kept going: *"You're not trying to gain weight, right?"* — crossing out the weight gainer. *"And you aren't trying to boost your testosterone, right?"* — crossing that out too. Then he prescribed from what remained. **She bought without hesitation.** *"I went out of my way to cross out what she didn't need, and this built enough goodwill to upsell what she did."*

**The four tactics in three industries:**

| | Massage therapist | Dog food | Digital product |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unsell** | "We have a lymphatic massage, but you're not pregnant or just out of surgery, right? So we can cross that out." | "You won't need this small bag or the puppy stuff — you've got a big dog. And you don't need these vitamins, the food already contains them." | "You don't need all eight courses yet. You just need to solve X, Y and Z. I'll send you free stuff that solves X and Y." |
| **Prescribe** | "Since your shoulder hurts, we'll heat you up first, then hit your trigger points, then do dynamic stretches." | "Give your dog one of these joint chews at each meal, and every 90 days one of these wafers for heartworms. Bring him back next month — let's book it now." | "To solve Z you'll want to do the course this particular way. Can you put an hour a day toward it? Great — that'll prevent other Z problems later." |
| **A/B** | "Would you rather do it before work or on the way home?" | "Does your dog prefer beef or chicken?" | "Direct message or phone support?" then "Start today or Monday?" |
| **Card on file** | "Want to just use the card on file?" | "Want to just use the card on file?" | "Want to just use the card on file?" |

**The Economist Play, worked.** In the late 1990s The Economist launched a digital subscription while wanting to protect print:

| Version | Options | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Original | A: digital $59 · B: digital + print $125 | **Print sales plummeted** — everyone took the cheap option |
| Fixed | A: digital $59 · B: **print only $125** · C: digital + print $125 | **Customers took C** |

Adding a decoy priced identically to the bundle made the bundle the obvious choice. *"So long as you price the options to preserve your margins, you make the customer's choice easy and sell both."*

## Key Takeaways
1. Unsell → prescribe → A/B → card on file. In that order.
2. Never ask a yes/no question at the point of purchase.
3. Prescribe in detail, integrated with what they already own. Detail outsells persuasion.
4. Add a nudge to every A/B, and nudge harder on what you want to move.
5. Let employees unsell — the goodwill funds the upsell.
6. Charge for guarantees, warranties, and insurance instead of giving them away.
7. Use a same-priced decoy to make a bundle the obvious choice.
