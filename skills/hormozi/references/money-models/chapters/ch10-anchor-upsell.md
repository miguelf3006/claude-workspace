# Chapter 10: Upsell Offers — The Anchor Upsell

## Core Idea
Present the **really expensive thing first**. Everything you offer afterward reads as cheap by comparison — even things far above the customer's original budget.

> *"The only thing worse than making a $1,000 offer to a person with a $100 budget is making a $100 offer to someone with a $1,000 budget."*

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Anchor Upsell Steps**:
  1. **Present the anchor** — the really expensive thing
  2. **Get the gasp** — the visible reaction is the signal that the anchor landed
  3. **Ease down gracefully**, giving them a face-saving reason to move to the next tier
  4. **Sell the accessories** — after the anchor, add-ons read as trivial
- **The Face-Saving Question**: The move that makes the descent graceful rather than humiliating — *"Do you care about the designer much?"* It hands the customer a reason for choosing less that has nothing to do with money.
- **Primary vs. Secondary Features**: The suit is the **primary** feature; the material, designer, and label are **secondary**. After anchoring, you offer a version that keeps the primary feature and trades away the secondary ones.

## Key Concepts
- **The anchor doesn't have to sell.** Its entire job is to reset the customer's sense of what a number means. It can even be something you rarely sell.
- **Accessories are where anchoring pays twice.** After a large anchor, the add-on stack barely registers.
- **The gasp is diagnostic.** No reaction means the anchor wasn't high enough for that customer.

## Mental Models
- **Anchoring resets the unit of measurement, not the budget.** The customer doesn't have more money — they have a new reference point for what "expensive" means.
- **Never let the customer feel poor.** The whole craft is in the descent. Deliver the reason before they have to invent one.
- **Budgets are guesses.** A $500 budget became a $2,500 purchase because the reference point moved, not because the person's finances changed.

## Anti-patterns
- **Leading with the cheap option**: You've capped the sale at that number before the conversation starts.
- **Anchoring without an exit**: An anchor with no graceful step down leaves the customer embarrassed and looking for the door.
- **Reading the blush as a lost sale**: It's the moment the technique starts working, not the moment it fails.

## Worked Example
**The suit shop, 2016.** After five years "showerless in sweats and a tank top," a fashionable friend told Hormozi that *"businessmen don't wear tank tops"* and sent him to a local suit shop. He **budgeted $500** — a big purchase at the time.

| Step | What happened |
|---|---|
| Small talk & measurements | Owner grabs **two** suits off the rack |
| Suit 1 on | *"How's it look? How do you feel?"* — Hormozi felt "cool, like a rich guy," stopped listening to the accessory talk |
| **The gasp** | He flipped the price tag: **$16,000.** His face turned red. He tried to hide it and failed |
| **The face-saver** | The owner: *"Do you care about the designer much?"* — *"Not at all."* |
| **The descent** | Almost before he finished replying, the owner draped the second suit over his shoulders: *"Try this on for size."* **$2,200.** Sigh of relief. *"Yeah, this works. I'll take this one."* |
| **The accessories** | Socks, handkerchief, shirt — **another $300**. "After seeing the $16,000 price tag, everything seemed cheap." |

**Result: a $500 budget became a $2,500 purchase.** *"Looking back, this wasn't the owner's first rodeo. He was a real pro."*

Notice the mechanics, each of which is transferable:
- The owner grabbed **two** suits at the start — the sequence was planned before a word was said
- He never named a price out loud; **the customer discovered it**, which made the anchor feel like information rather than a pitch
- The face-saving question arrived **within seconds of the blush** — the recovery was faster than the embarrassment
- The second suit was already in hand; there was no gap in which the customer could leave
- Accessories came **last**, when the reference point was at its highest

## Key Takeaways
1. Lead with the expensive thing. Its job is to move the reference point, not to sell.
2. Watch for the gasp — it tells you the anchor landed and it's time to descend.
3. Supply the face-saving reason yourself, immediately, and make it about preference not price.
4. Keep the primary feature, trade away the secondary ones on the way down.
5. Sell accessories after the anchor, never before.
6. Plan the whole sequence before the conversation starts.
