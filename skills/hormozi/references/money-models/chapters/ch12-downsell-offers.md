# Chapter 12: Downsell Offers — What to Offer When They Say No

## Core Idea
**Any offer you make after someone says no is a downsell.** Downselling tweaks the offer to find the highest-value solution for the customer's budget — by changing **how they pay** or **what they get.** Never by simply dropping the price.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Two Downsell Dimensions**: change **how they pay** (balance what they pay now against what they pay over time) or **what they get** (quantity, quality, or something different).
- **The Rules of Downselling**:
  1. **They said no to *this* offer, not all offers.** "Just because they rejected this offer does not mean they rejected you… No means no for this thing, not no for everything."
  2. **Downsells are trades.** "If you're going to give something, get something."
  3. **Personalize, don't pressure.** Figure out what they like and don't like, then offer more of what they like and less of what they don't, with a price to match.
  4. **Don't drop your price just to get somebody to buy.** That's not downselling — that's discounting.
- **The Three Downsell Processes**:
  - **Payment plan downsell** *(how they pay)* — same product, cost spread across an upfront portion and scheduled payments
  - **Trial with penalty** *(how they pay)* — free access conditional on terms; they only pay if they *don't* meet them
  - **Feature downsell** *(what they get)* — lower the price by removing a feature, so you're not discounting
- **The Trial with Penalty Structure** — the mirror image of win-your-money-back:
  - *Win your money back*: customers get money back **if they meet the terms**
  - *Trial with penalty*: customers only pay **if they don't meet the terms**
  - *"It's not 'here's my thing, see if you like it.' It's 'here's my thing, you get it for free so long as you do this stuff — which makes you a perfect fit for my next offer. And if you don't, you pay for it.'"*
- **The Five-Step Trial Downsell**:
  1. **Offer the trial last** — only after they've clearly declined the main offer. *"That sure is a pickle. I'll tell you what — how about we just get you started for free? Would you be okay with that? We can help you out, and if you like it, you can stay. Let me get your ID and we can get started."*
  2. **Always get a credit card** — hold the ID and motion for the card: *"What card do you want to use?"* If they balk: *"That's just how we've always done it."* If they still refuse, wish them a lovely day.
  3. **Always sell staying and paying** — *"If this program got you the result, will you stay long term?"* If no, there's no point in the trial.
  4. **Explain the fees AFTER getting the card** — *"We'll do our part so long as you do yours. That's fair, right? Now I just ask that you bet on yourself. If you miss or skip stuff, your results suffer, so we charge to keep you on track. If you follow through, you get all this for free."* **Explaining before the card produces more resistance.** Have customers initial separately next to the fee clauses to force salespeople to explain them.
  5. **Make check-ins required** — and they double as upsell opportunities: *"You agree to attend each of the three check-ins. The first we do X so you benefit; the second Y; the third Z. Obviously we charge if you miss these, because it's the only way we can get you results."*

## Key Concepts
- **The customer-count math**: If you normally close 3 of 10 on the attraction offer and downsell another 4 into a trial with penalty, then upsell 3 of those — **you go from 3 sales to 6. Double the customers.** *"If you only have one offer, you lose everyone who says no."*
- **Fees: broken up or lump?** On a $500 product with 10 required actions, Hormozi would rather bill **$50 per mess-up** than $500 on the first. But if missing once genuinely wrecks their result, make the fee reflect that. Both work.
- **Upselling from a trial** — three outcomes: **they like it** (meet anyway; offer longer-term or higher-value), **they hate it** (ask what they'd have liked different, say they're totally right, be angry *at yourself* — *"only one person can be angry, and it needs to be you"* — then offer the higher-level thing; **about half buy**), **they didn't use it** (reach out repeatedly, offer to waive the fee if they meet; *"a small fee isn't worth a one-star review"*).
- **Tuning the trial**: nobody takes it → lower requirements or penalties. They take it but don't follow through → emphasize how fees help them and make sales meetings mandatory. They don't stay on the back end → emphasize staying and paying, deliver better, and make sure the back end fits the front end.
- **Just call it a free trial.** "No one wants to be penalized." If asked why you do free trials this way: *"This is just how we've always done it"* or *"people get the best results this way."*
- **Which downsell where**: **pay less now or pay more later** for physical products and one-time services · **trial with penalty** for recurring products or services. *And Hormozi has only made trials work in businesses where the customer has to do work to get results.*
- **Discounts get cards on file**: If free-plus-card feels weird, a very low price justifies the ask and confirms the card works. *First month for $1, then $X/month recurring.*

## Mental Models
- **"Offer something different for less. You just can't offer the same thing for less."** The whole ethical and practical line, in one sentence.
- **The feature downsell insight: guarantees are features.** Customers only see the value of a guarantee once you offer to remove it.
- **Downselling is 100 ways to offer the stuff you already have** — not an excuse to build a hundred businesses' worth of products.

## Anti-patterns
- **The descending price ladder**: A friend was offered car insurance at $5,000, said no, and watched the salesman walk it down to **$400.** She still said no — *"at first because it was too much money, but by the end because I didn't trust the guy. Then I wondered: was he ripping me off on the car too?"* She no longer wanted to buy the car either. **"You trade trust for a buck. Not worth it."** Had he offered *different* insurance for less, he'd likely have kept the trust and the sale.
- **In-the-moment price cuts**: Customers talk. If someone finds out another person got the same thing for less just because you were scared of losing a sale, you'll upset people — and it's an ethical problem. Test prices deliberately and in advance instead.
- **Selling someone who won't put a card down**: *"They complain more and they convert less. Not worth the hassle."*

## Worked Example
**The first payment plan, August 2013.** First real month in business, one month's rent left, and he'd never gotten a stranger to pay him. A lead walked in and said she couldn't afford it:

> *"Okay — when do you get paid?"* — "The first."
> *"Just put half down now and half when you get paid."* — "I can't afford that either."
> *"Do you really want to do this program?"* — "Yeah, I do."
> *"What if you do three payments and put a third down today?"* — "I still can't."
> *"What can you do?"* — **"Honestly, nothing. But I can pay for the whole thing on the 1st."**

His rent was due on the 5th. *"Sounds good. Just give me your card and I'll charge it on the second."* Two weeks later the card ran. **"My first ever payment plan, a success."**

**Why payment plans are a gamble:** they make money **one** way (more customers who complete their payments) and lose money **two** ways (people cancel before you turn a profit — and worst of all, **people who would have paid in full take a plan and cancel early**).

**The trial with penalty, discovered.** Leila spent months on sales calls with HR companies and chose one that "looked like all the others." Why?

> "They said if I did their training, I'd get free onboarding. But if I skipped the training, I'd have to pay for it."
> — *"So what did you do?"* "I went through the training, of course."
> — *"And now?"* "Now I can actually use the complicated software. **It forced me to learn, and now I don't want to learn anyone else's complicated software. So we're sticking with them.**"

**Templates:**

| Business | Trial with penalty terms |
|---|---|
| **B2C** | *28-Day Kick That Habit Blueprint.* To get it free and avoid the fee: attend all consulting calls · post progress weekly in the group · journal daily in the app · attend feedback and transformation sessions *(upsell opportunities)* |
| **B2B** | *5-Day Get Your First Five Customers Challenge.* Send 100 outbound messages/day · report stats · attend daily training · post homework daily · attend your graduation call *(upsell opportunity)* |
| **Software** | *$500 onboarding, then $90/month.* You don't pay the $500 upfront, but you must attend onboarding (three 60-minute Zoom calls — *upsell opportunities*), do the homework, activate your employer profile, and get employees set up by the end of the third call. Otherwise you pay the fee |

**The feature downsell — the guarantee discovery.** An operator tripled his close rate from **25% to 75%**, and *more people bought the main thing.* Not payment plans ("take too long"), not discounts ("devalue my product"). Instead: *"I lower the price, but I justify it by cutting a feature."* The feature he cut was **his full money-back guarantee.**

> "When we get a price objection, we ask: **'If you don't want the option to get your money back, you can pay less. Or you can keep your money-back guarantee. Which would you prefer?'** Once they understand what they'd give up, they often say *screw it, I'd rather keep the guarantee.*"

*"So they only see the value of the guarantee after you remove it — and that also explains why so many more are buying the main thing."*

## Key Takeaways
1. Every post-"no" offer is a downsell. Have them ready before the call.
2. Change how they pay or what they get. Never the same thing for less money.
3. A trial with penalty converts the people a plain free trial loses — and its terms should be the behaviours that make great long-term customers.
4. Card first, fees second. Order matters more than wording.
5. Build required check-ins into the trial; they're scheduled upsell slots.
6. Cut a feature to lower a price — customers discover the feature's value in the removal.
7. Downselling a "no" can literally double your customer count.
