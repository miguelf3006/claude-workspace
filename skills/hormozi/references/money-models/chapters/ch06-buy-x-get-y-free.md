# Chapter 6: Attraction Offers — Buy X, Get Y Free

## Core Idea
When customers buy something, they get other stuff free. **The more free stuff they get and the higher its value, the better it works** — because free drives far more interest than an arithmetically identical discount.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Reframe Mechanic**: With more than one item to sell, you can make the discount value large enough that it covers the price of more stuff — converting a discount offer into a **free** offer at the same total price.

| Structure | Price paid | How it reads |
|---|---|---|
| 3 t-shirts at $10 each | $30 | A normal purchase |
| **1 t-shirt for $30, get 2 free** | $30 | **Two free shirts** |
| 3 t-shirts at $6.67 each (33% off) | $20 | A discount |
| **1 t-shirt for $20, get 2 free** | $20 | **Two free shirts** |

- **The Duration Version** (18 months of service, same price, escalating appeal):
  - *Good*: Buy 12 months, get 6 free — $1,800
  - *Better*: Buy 9 months, get 9 free — $1,800
  - *Best*: **Buy 6 months, get 12 free — $1,800**
  - Everyone pays the same for the same service. The third is most compelling because **it has the most free stuff.**
- **The Governing Rule**: **Always give more free things than paid things.** "Buy 10, get 2 free" is far weaker than "Buy 2, get 10 free."

## Key Concepts
- **Raise prices before giving stuff away, to preserve profit.** "If you use this to attract customers, it will work. And since it will work, you need to make money. So permanently raise prices to accommodate the discount. **Don't lie — actually raise your prices.**" Bonus: plenty of people will take the doubled price as-is, which breaks your own limiting beliefs about pricing.
- **The free things can differ from the paid things.** Beginners match them, but you can mix. If socks carry a $10 value, *"buy one shirt for $10, get $20 of free socks"* may read better than shirt-for-shirt.
- **More cheap free things can beat fewer expensive ones.** For the cost of one free shirt you might give three pairs of socks — and *"buy one shirt, get three things free"* often tests better. Worth running head to head.
- **It lengthens customer lifetime.** If normal customers stay three months, "buy 2 get 2 free" keeps them four — which is four months of opportunities to make more offers and provide more value.
- **Prepaid customers are your best buyers.** "A lot of people don't want to make more offers to customers who prepay. This is a mistake. **These are the people who spend the most money.** They may have prepaid months ago — their wallets have been refreshed with new money that is dying to make it into your pocket. Don't get in the way."

## Mental Models
- **"More people know the value of *free* than the value of one shirt."** That asymmetry is the entire arbitrage.
- **If customers only buy once, make them buy big.** Boot Factory served tourists making a single lifetime purchase — so the right move is to make that purchase as large as possible, backed by real value.
- **A discount needs a reason; free doesn't.** Businesses with one thing to sell lean on holidays and seasons to justify sales. With more than one thing, you don't need an excuse.

## Anti-patterns
- **Running it without the cash discipline**: "If you get a whole year's worth of payments in a month, make sure you can deliver for the whole year. Budget the correct amount to service customers for the duration. **Don't be a goon and buy yourself a house with the cash meant for servicing your customers.** Selling stuff you can't deliver on breaks the law and ruins your reputation."
- **Matching free to paid one-for-one**: "Buy one, get one" is the weakest version of the play.
- **Uncapped fast-cash offers to your existing base**: If you run this to existing recurring customers for a cash pop, **limit it to about 10% of your customers** so recurring cash flow stays healthy.

## Worked Example
**Boot Factory, downtown Nashville.** A neon sign with a cowboy boot arrow, in a district where bars and shops churned constantly — but this store kept growing. The offer: **buy one, get two free.**

Hormozi went in and found a pair marked down twice to **$600 for the pair** — for ordinary-looking boots. Then it clicked: *"They charged three times the price for a single pair of boots because they came with two more pairs."*

| Framing | Price | Customer reaction |
|---|---|---|
| "Come buy boots at a fair price" — $200/pair | $600 for three | A normal store |
| **"Buy one pair for $600, get two pairs free"** | $600 for three | **A free offer** |

Identical economics. And because the store sat in the middle of a street full of cowboy-themed bars, **bachelorette parties filed in for matching boots** — a group of buyers for whom three pairs is a feature, not a burden.

**Hormozi's own version:** his service businesses used to take a full year to make their money back. Switching to **"buy 6 months, get 6 months free"** attracted far more customers than the original month-to-month offer — *and got paid upfront for it.*

**The fast-cash variant:** with an existing recurring business needing cash quickly, offer "buy 10, get 2 free" to current customers **at their current price.** Many will happily take it. Cap it at ~10% of the base for a good cash pop that doesn't gut recurring revenue.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reframe a discount as free stuff. Same money, far more interest.
2. Always give more free items than paid items.
3. Raise prices *before* running it, honestly and permanently.
4. Mix categories — cheaper free items can outperform expensive ones.
5. Use duration versions (buy 6, get 12) to extend lifetime and collect cash upfront.
6. Budget the collected cash against your delivery obligation. This is where businesses die.
7. Keep selling to prepaid customers — they're your highest spenders.
