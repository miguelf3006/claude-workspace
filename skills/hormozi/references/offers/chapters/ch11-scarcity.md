# Chapter 11: Enhancing the Offer — Scarcity

## Core Idea
Scarcity is a function of **quantity**. Fixed, publicly stated limits trigger fear of loss — which is a stronger motivator than desire for gain — and unlock pricing power without changing the product.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Types of Scarcity**:
  1. Limited supply of seats/slots (in general, or over a period)
  2. Limited supply of bonuses
  3. Never available again
- **Four Scarcity Mechanisms for Services**:
  1. **Total business cap** — "My agency only services 25 customers total." Creates a waiting list; periodically increase capacity 10–20%, then cap again. Works best for your highest tiers.
  2. **Growth rate cap** — "We only accept 5 new clients per week and 3 spots are taken. I have 6 more calls this week." Banks on your real weekly capacity.
  3. **Cohort cap** — "We take 100 clients four times a year. We open the doors, then close them." Adds operational cadence *and* legitimate sales scarcity.
  4. **Honest scarcity** — the easiest and most ethical: define what you can actually handle, then advertise it. "We're three-quarters to capacity this week." "We're 81% to capacity."
- **Extreme Scarcity (pro tip)**: Sell a very limited supply of one-on-one access via whatever medium you personally don't hate — DM, email, phone, voice memo, Zoom. Cap at a tiny number, price very high. "The cream of the crop, the 1% of 1% will adjust and take action."
- **Once You're Out, You Can Never Come Back**: Cap a service level and state that leavers can't return. Makes people think extra hard about leaving. Works best in small groups; loses teeth as groups get large.

## Key Concepts
- **Implied demand**: Why an authority (a doctor), a celebrity (Oprah), or a celebrity-authority (Dr. Oz, Dr. Phil) can charge egregious rates — people assume high demand for their time, therefore low supply, therefore expensive.
- **Rarity × value compounding**: Two components to premium price — how rare the solvers are, and the actual value provided. Specialized consultants get paid millions to solve problems worth tens of millions.
- **Supply = 1**: A specialized problem has few possible solvers; often the prospect perceives only one.
- **Scarcity implies social proof**: If you're 81% to capacity, a decent number of people already chose you — and the closer to full, the faster remaining spots disappear.
- **Sold-out announcements**: Telling everyone you sold out is part of what makes it work. It supplies social proof and removes choice, which raises desire.

## Mental Models
- **Fear of loss is stronger than desire for gain.** Humans are far more motivated to hoard a scarce resource than to act on something that could help them.
- **Better to sell out consistently than to over-order and fail to create scarcity.** The strategy compounds over repetition — one of the few in the marketing arsenal that does. Monthly is the sweet spot for most companies doing this with regularity.
- **You draw the line where "full" is.** Capacity limits are your choice; that's what makes honest scarcity available to everyone.

## Anti-patterns
- **Phony scarcity**: Fake countdowns and invented limits cost credibility and make you look like every other wannabe marketer.
- **Over-supplying so you never sell out**: Destroys the compounding effect and trains the market that there's always more.
- **Using cap-and-never-return in large groups**: The tactic weakens as group size grows.

## Worked Example
**Chanel.** A brand that has held insane margins and pricing for over a century sends only **one to two of each piece to each store**. Every store has a different selection; every item is the last or second-to-last in stock. This lets them price far above market and converts browsing impulses into purchases.

**Scarcity applied to a *free* lead magnet.** Hormozi's own illustration: if he says there's a free checklist summarizing the book, you might go download it. But if he says **the page only allows 20 new people to download it each week** — and when you try, it has already run out — you join a notification list. When the alert arrives you drop what you're doing and click, because you don't want to miss out again. A neat free download becomes a desirable thing not everyone has access to, and *you're far more likely to actually consume it.* All from controlling supply.

**The $50,000 day he turned down.** After speaking at an event, two people separately offered $50,000 for a day of his time — they were stuck under $1M/month in an education business while he was doing $1M/week in the same model. He declined, because he was making more than $50,000/day in profit and didn't want the distraction. The lesson he draws: *"There's a special type of zero-fucks-given vibe that's hard to replicate when you truly do not need a person's money or even want it."*

## Key Takeaways
1. Pick one or more scarcity mechanisms and state the limit publicly.
2. Honest scarcity is the easiest and safest: define real capacity, then advertise it.
3. Always sell out — and always announce that you sold out.
4. Scarcity compounds across repetitions; run it about monthly, not constantly.
5. Rarity and value multiply. Be the perceived supply of one for a valuable, specialized problem.
6. A tiny, expensive, access-based tier is the fastest money in the book — and often the best clients.
