# Chapter 3: Lead Magnets — Getting Leads to Engage

## Core Idea
A **lead magnet is a complete solution to a narrow problem** — free or low-cost, valuable enough that you *could* charge for it, and which, once solved, reveals the next problem that your core offer solves.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Seven Steps to an Effective Lead Magnet**:
  1. **Figure out the problem you want to solve and who to solve it for**
  2. **Figure out how to solve it**
  3. **Figure out how to deliver it**
  4. **Test what to name it**
  5. **Make it easy to consume**
  6. **Make it darn good**
  7. **Make it easy for them to tell you they want more**
- **The Problem–Solution Cycle**: Every problem has a solution; every solution reveals more problems. Smaller cycles sit inside larger cycles. Pick a problem that is **narrow and meaningful**, solve it — and make sure the newly revealed problem is one your **core offer** solves for money.
- **The Three Types of Lead Magnet** (step 2):
  1. **Reveal their problem** (think diagnosis) — best when the problem worsens the longer you wait. Website speed test, posture analysis, termite inspection.
  2. **Samples and trials** — full but brief access to your core offer, limited by uses or time. Best when the core offer is a *recurring solution to a recurring problem*. This is how Costco sells more food than other stores.
  3. **One step of a multi-step process** — best when the core offer solves a complex problem. Free garage-door sealant coat one of three; free finance calculators that reveal the effort of doing it yourself.
- **The Four Delivery Vehicles** (step 3): **software** (a tool does a job — spreadsheet, calculator, dashboard) · **information** (courses, lessons, interviews, keynotes, pitfalls, hacks) · **services** (free work — adjustment, website audit, first sealant coat) · **physical products** (posture chart, supplement, sample bottle, boxing gloves).
  - 3 types × 4 vehicles = **up to 12 lead magnets for a single narrow problem.** Make as many versions as you can and rotate them — keeps advertising fresh at low effort and shows which win.
- **Good CTAs Have Two Parts** (step 7): (1) **what to do** — clear, simple, direct ("call now," not "don't delay"); (2) **reasons to do it right now** — scarcity, urgency, or the fraternity-party-planner reason.

## Key Concepts
- **Even free stuff has a cost**: People will give you time before money, but time is still a cost. *"If your lead magnet isn't worth their time, it's overpriced."* If they think it's worth their time, they'll think your core offer is worth their money.
- **Give away the secrets, sell the implementation**: The goal is to provide more value *before* they buy than the cost of your core offer.
- **Ethical scarcity is reality**: If you can't handle more than five new customers a week, say so. Draw attention to the natural limits already in your business.
- **The fraternity party planner** (favorite CTA reason): Make up a reason. Harvard's line-cutting experiment found any reason beats no reason — sensible reasons work better, but the word *because* is doing real work.
- **The four jobs of a good lead magnet**: (1) engages ideal customers when they see it, (2) gets more people to engage than the core offer alone, (3) is valuable enough that they consume it, (4) makes the right people more likely to buy.

## Mental Models
- **Salty pretzels at a bar.** The pretzels solve hunger (narrow problem) and reveal thirst (solved by a drink, for money). The pretzels cost something; the drink revenue covers it and nets profit.
- **Be a legal drug dealer.** Give away the strongest hit first. This is why you never dilute a lead magnet or give away sucky fluff — your lead magnet is the first hit; the next one they pay for.
- **The dressing room is all three types at once.** You try something on (trial), you discover you need something different (reveals a problem), and a good salesperson adds "want pants with that?" (step one of a multi-step process). Aim for a lead magnet that does all three.
- **A person who pays with their time now is more likely to pay with their money later.**

## Anti-patterns
- **Giving away sucky fluff to protect your secrets**: People who might have become customers conclude you suck, buy elsewhere, and tell others not to buy from you. A vicious cycle.
- **One format only**: You forfeit the 2–4× of people who consume differently.
- **Clever over clear CTAs**: "Don't be clever, be clear."
- **Overthinking the problem selection**: Pick narrow, solve it, check that your core offer solves the next one. "Don't overthink it."

## Worked Example
**Step 4 in practice — how this book got named.** Hormozi didn't know what to title it, so he tested:

| Test | Options | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Headline R1 | advertising vs. promotion | — |
| Headline R2 | advertising vs. leads | — |
| Headline R3 | marketing vs. **leads** | leads |
| Image | real vs. cartoon | — |
| Subhead R1 | "…get more people to want to buy your stuff" vs. "…get **strangers** to want to buy your stuff" | strangers |
| Subhead R2 | "…get **more** strangers…" vs. "…get strangers…" | remove "more" |
| Subhead R4 | "Get strangers to want to buy your stuff" vs. "**How to** get strangers to want to buy your stuff" | **"How to" won overwhelmingly** |

Two words added, one word removed — large differences. Ogilvy's rule is why: *"When you have written your headline, you have spent 80 cents of your advertising dollar."* Five times more people read the headline than any other part. Test in this order: **headline → images → sub-headline**. No audience? Run polls, post on every platform asking for a "1 or 2," or just message people. *"There's always a way."*

**Step 5 in practice.** $100M Offers has a near-perfect **one-quarter split across ebook, physical, audiobook, and free video** on acquisition.com. One format would have forfeited 3–4× the readers.

**The lead magnet economics** (why free costs less than direct):

| | Advertise core offer | Advertise free lead magnet |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to get someone on a call | $1,000 | $75 ad + $25 delivery = **$100** |
| Close rate | 1 in 3 | 1 in 10 |
| **Cost to acquire a customer** | **$3,000** | **$1,000** |
| Profit per customer | $10,000 | $10,000 |
| Return | 3.3 : 1 | **10 : 1** |

Same budget, **3× the business.** And note the counterintuitive part: a **$25 delivery budget lets you provide far more value than a $0 budget** — which is exactly why experienced operators beat newcomers here.

## Key Takeaways
1. Solve a narrow, meaningful problem completely — and make sure the next problem is your core offer's.
2. Pick from three types (reveal / sample / one step) × four vehicles (software / info / service / physical). Rotate versions.
3. Test the name before anything else. Headline first, then images, then sub-headline.
4. Package in every format you can — it's the cheapest 2–4× available.
5. Make it as good as your paid stuff. 99% won't buy, but they'll build or destroy your reputation.
6. Always end with a clear CTA plus a reason to act now, and do it often.
7. A paid delivery budget on a free lead magnet usually *lowers* customer acquisition cost.
