# Chapter 3: Pricing — The Commodity Problem

## Core Idea
Commoditization is not caused by competitors; it is caused by presenting an offer the prospect can score on the same scale as someone else's. Price competition is the symptom; comparability is the disease.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Grow-or-Die Growth Equation**: New clients/month × lifetime value = revenue ceiling. 10 clients/mo × $1,000 LTV = $10,000/mo maximum, forever, until one of the two terms moves.
  - When to use: Setting a growth target, or explaining why more hustle isn't producing more revenue.
  - How: Identify which term you are actually able to move this quarter, then move only that one.
- **Commoditized vs. Differentiated**:
  - Commoditized → price-driven purchase → race to the bottom.
  - Differentiated → value-driven purchase → sell in a category of one with no comparison.
- **The Commoditization Dilemma**: Once a client sees a cheaper version of the same thing, you face a forced choice — lose the client (and by extension the rest, and future prospects) or stay "competitive" until margins vanish. Both branches lose. The only escape is to leave the comparison set entirely.

## Key Concepts
- **Market efficiency**: The equilibrium where competition has driven margins to the minimum that keeps the lights on.
- **Value discrepancy**: The gap a client perceives when they see a similar product cheaper — the trigger for switching providers.
- **Price-driven purchase**: A decision made by ranking comparable options on cost.
- **Value-driven purchase**: A decision made by assessing worth in isolation, because no ranking is possible.
- **Business terms that matter**: gross profit (revenue − direct cost of servicing one more customer); LTV / LTGP (gross profit × purchases over the lifetime). Indirect costs — admin, software, rent — are excluded from LTV.

## Mental Models
- **Think of commoditization as *self-inflicted*.** "They're all making the same offer" is why they all look the same. Change the offer and the sameness disappears.
- **Use the growth equation to kill busywork.** If an activity doesn't move new clients, purchase value, or purchase frequency, it isn't growth.
- **Maintenance is decay.** Flat revenue against a 9% market is a real-terms decline.

## Anti-patterns
- **Being "reasonable"**: The pay-us-to-work offer is reasonable, easily duplicated, and therefore fatally comparable.
- **Excluding indirect costs and then acting rich**: LTV omits rent, admin, and software. Do not confuse it with what you can afford to spend.
- **Using revenue-based LTV**: Inflates your acquisition budget and hides negative unit economics.

## Worked Example
The cap calculation Hormozi uses to end "we just need to work harder" conversations:

```
10 new clients / month × $1,000 lifetime value = $10,000 / month maximum revenue
```

Nothing you do to the calendar changes that number. Only three moves exist: sell more clients per month at suitable margins, raise profit per purchase, or raise the number of purchases. Increasing effort while both terms are fixed produces a busier version of the same ceiling — the mechanical reason hard-working commoditized businesses stall.

## Key Takeaways
1. You cannot out-work a comparability problem; you must exit the comparison set.
2. Three growth levers, and only three: more customers, higher purchase value, more purchases.
3. Define gross profit and LTGP precisely before making any pricing decision.
4. Competing on price is a forced-loss dilemma with no winning branch.
5. Differentiation must be structural (built into the offer), not rhetorical (explained in marketing).
