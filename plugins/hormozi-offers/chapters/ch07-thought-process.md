# Chapter 7: Value — The Offer Thought Process

## Core Idea
Offer creation is a **divergent** thinking task, not a convergent one. School trained you to find the single right answer; offers reward generating many right answers and picking the one that's far more right.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Convergent vs. Divergent Problem Solving**:
  - *Convergent*: many known variables, unchanging conditions, one answer. Think math. Binary — right or wrong. It's what school teaches because it's easy to grade.
  - *Divergent*: multiple variables (known and unknown), dynamic conditions, multiple answers — with one answer far more right than the rest.
  - When to use: Every offer-building session. "Life will pay you for your ability to solve using a divergent thought process."
- **The Brick Exercise** (120 seconds): List as many uses of a brick as you can.
  - How: Set a timer for 120 seconds and write. Then interrogate the *constraints you silently assumed*: How big is the brick — a stick of gum, standard 3⅝ × 2¼ × 8 in, or 2 ft × 2 ft × 6 ft? What is it made of — plastic, gold, clay, wood, metal? What shape — holes, interlocking divots?
  - Why it works: The number of solutions explodes once you stop treating unstated attributes as fixed. Offer components behave the same way.
- **Building Blocks Principle**: Every offer has building blocks — pieces that, combined, make it irresistible. Find the customer's desire first, then devise how many ways you can create value with what you have.

## Key Concepts
- **Divergent solution space**: Multiple valid answers of unequal quality — the goal is the one that is *way* more right.
- **Assumed constraints**: The unexamined attributes (size, material, shape) that collapse your option space before you start.
- **Desire-first design**: Establish what the customer wants, then work backward through combinations. Not: build a thing, then look for who wants it.

## Mental Models
- **Think of a brick as a variable, not an object.** Anything you treat as fixed is a constraint you chose.
- **Use divergence for generation, convergence for selection.** Generate widely without judging, then converge on the strongest combination.
- **If you're stuck on an offer, you're probably still solving convergently** — hunting for *the* answer instead of producing twenty.

## Anti-patterns
- **Grading yourself while generating**: Convergent instincts kill option volume. Separate the two modes.
- **Accepting the default form of your product**: The "standard brick" assumption. Most offers are dull because nobody questioned the shape, size, or material of what's being sold.
- **Building from the product outward**: Starting with what you have instead of what they want.

## Worked Example
**The brick list** (Hormozi's own, after questioning the constraints):

paperweight · door stop · building material · home for a fish in a fishbowl · plant holder (dirt in the holes) · trophy (whole brick) · rustic decoration (painted brick) · window breaker · mural made of tiny painted bricks · resistance-training weight · wedge under an uneven platform · pen holder · children's toy (Lego-style bricks) · flotation device (plastic brick) · payment for goods (gold brick) · stabilizer for leaning things · store of value (gold brick) · flagpole holder · seat (jumbo brick)

Note how many entries only became possible *after* changing an assumed attribute — plastic unlocks flotation, gold unlocks currency and store-of-value, jumbo unlocks seating, tiny unlocks murals. That is the transferable move: for each component of your offer, ask what changes if its size, material, or shape changes.

**The convergent contrast** (so you can feel the difference): Three salespeople each take 100 calls/month; 4 calls produce 1 sale; you need 110 sales. 100 ÷ 4 = 25 closes per rep; 110 ÷ 25 = 4.4 → you need 5 reps, you have 3, so hire 2. One answer, provably right — and nothing like offer design.

## Key Takeaways
1. Offers are built divergently. Generate many combinations before evaluating any.
2. Interrogate assumed attributes — size, material, form, delivery, sequence, who does the work.
3. Start from the customer's desire, then combine what you have to serve it.
4. Multiple right answers exist; your job is to find the one that's dramatically more right.
5. Schooling optimized you for convergent thinking. Deliberately switch modes before an offer session.
