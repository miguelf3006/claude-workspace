# Chapter 3: Attraction Offers — Win Your Money Back

## Core Idea
Set a goal for the customer, tell them how to reach it, and **if they reach it they get their money back** — as cash or store credit. They bet on themselves; you get paid, get them results, and get marketing material.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Win Your Money Back Structure**: Customer puts money down. If they *do the stuff*, *get the result*, or *both*, they get it back as cash or store credit.
- **The Three Qualification Options**:
  1. **Results** — if they get the result, they win their money back. *(Making $X a month, getting Y customers, losing Z lbs.)* They bet on their own ability to reach the goal.
  2. **Actions** — if they do what you ask, they win regardless of outcome. *(Attend all sessions, calls, meetings; log progress; take pictures; do assigned homework.)* They bet on their ability to follow directions.
  3. **Both** — they must follow directions **and** get the result. They bet on their ability to follow directions *and* on your directions working.
  - **Why "both" is usually right**: "Often people wanting to achieve a goal have too few skills to do it. Even if they bet on themselves, they'd fail. By setting a good goal for them and showing how they reach it, they have a fighting chance."
- **The Requirement**: The results and actions must be **simple to track**.

## Key Concepts
- **Why it still makes money**: (1) Many won't qualify, even with realistic conditions. (2) Those who *do* qualify often stay as customers — **but only if they have something else to buy.** So **have an upsell ready to apply their winnings to.**
- **The refund benchmark**: From data across thousands of gyms, **about 10% of all customers will ask for their money back.** If you can't stomach that, don't run this offer.
- **Store credit vs. cash**: Testing showed **store credit and cash back attracted the same number of customers** — so you might as well offer store credit. If you still want to advertise it as free, pair it with an unconditional satisfaction guarantee. (Adding that guarantee never materially changed how many people wanted their money back.)
- **Make everyone a winner in private**: Announce winners privately so everyone stays surprised and grateful when you make the upsell.
- **Scale of the play**: "This offer has generated over $1 billion in sales industry-wide."

## Mental Models
- **The best goal-setting mechanism disguised as an offer.** "To this day, I have never seen a better way of setting up a program for results — a true win-win."
- **Calls and meetings become opportunities to make more offers.** Every required check-in is a scheduled upsell slot.
- **Don't take blood money.** "If someone doesn't want me to have their money, I want it less than they do. If a customer asks for a refund — entitled or not — I give it to them. Just focus on getting the next customer."

## Anti-patterns
- **Running it with no upsell ready**: The winners come back with credit and nothing to spend it on. You've created goodwill with no way to monetize it.
- **Untrackable conditions**: If you can't verify the actions or the result cleanly, the offer generates disputes instead of customers.
- **Running it when refunds would break you**: 10% asking for their money back is the planning assumption, not the worst case.

## Worked Example
**Where it came from.** June 2013, a room of gym owners. Danny had a prospect who wouldn't buy — he knew he needed it but said he needed more accountability. The prospect proposed his own deal:

> *"How about this? I give you $500. You train me for eight weeks, and if I hit my goal, I get my money back. But in return, you can use my results to market your business."*

Danny figured he wasn't going to buy anyway, so he sold him. The guy **hit the goal** — and then **used the refund to buy more training**. His before-and-after pictures brought in **13 referrals**. Danny started offering it to everyone: better results, customers loved it, and the free advertising brought in their friends and family.

**Templates across business types:**

| Type | Offer |
|---|---|
| **B2C** | *"Free 28-Day Blueprint. Deposit $X and get it all back if you: attend all consulting calls · post progress in the group once per week · journal daily in our app · attend your feedback session and transformation session."* |
| **B2B** | *"Five Customers in 5 Days Free Challenge. Deposit $X and get it all back if you: send 100 messages per day · report stats on messages sent · attend daily training · post finished homework in the group · attend the day-five consulting call."* |
| **Physical product** | *"Put a million miles on your car and get a free car. Buy a new car from us, drive it X miles, turn it in, take pictures, be in a press release — we'll credit your original purchase price toward your next car."* (An actual offer someone ran.) |

Note the design in both service examples: every condition is **an action that produces the result**, and several are **scheduled contact points** where the next offer gets made.

**Where it works best**: with new customers (steepest possible discount — 100%), current customers (mixes them in with new ones), and **previous customers** (bigger incentives bring them back). And with things people **start and quit** — starting businesses, learning new skills, losing weight, building fitness, beauty regimes, self-care, time management, mental health management — because it maintains motivation through the early pain of learning.

## Key Takeaways
1. Money down, earned back by actions, results, or both. "Both" produces the best outcomes.
2. Make every condition trackable and make the actions the things that actually cause success.
3. Have an upsell ready before you launch — winners need somewhere to put their credit.
4. Plan for ~10% requesting money back. Don't run it if that breaks you.
5. Store credit performs as well as cash and costs less. Test it.
6. Notify winners privately so the upsell lands as a surprise.
7. Refund anyone who asks, gracefully, and move on.
