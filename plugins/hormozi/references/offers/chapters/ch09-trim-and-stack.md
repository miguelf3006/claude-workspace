# Chapter 9: Creating Your Grand Slam Offer, Part II — Trim & Stack

## Core Idea
Generate every possible delivery vehicle for every solution, then **trim to only low-cost/high-value and high-cost/high-value items** and stack the survivors into a bundle whose stated value dwarfs the price.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Offer Creation Process, Steps 4–5**:
  4. **Create your solutions delivery vehicles (the "how")** — divergently list everything you *could* do to solve each problem, including things you'd never actually do. The goal is to jog you out of your default.
  5. **Trim and stack** — 5A: cut by cost/value. 5B: bundle the survivors into the ultimate high-value deliverable.
- **The Product Delivery Cheat Codes** (six axes for generating variations):
  1. **Level of personal attention**: one-on-one · small group · one-to-many
  2. **Level of effort expected from them**: DIY (do it yourself) · DWY (do it with you) · DFY (done for you)
  3. **If live, what medium**: in person · phone · email · text · Zoom · chat
  4. **If recorded, how consumed**: audio · video · written
  5. **Response speed and hours**: 24/7 · 9–5 · within 5 min · within an hour · within 24 hours
  6. **The 10× / 1⁄10th Test**: If they paid you 10× your price (or $100,000), what would you provide? If they paid 1⁄10th and you had to make it *more* valuable, how would you still make them succeed?
- **The Trim Rule**: Remove **high-cost/low-value** first, then **low-cost/low-value**. What remains is low-cost/high-value and high-cost/high-value. Judge "value" by running the four value-equation questions on each item.
- **Sales-to-Fulfilment Continuum**: Lower what you must do → harder to sell. Do as much as possible → easy to sell, hard to fulfil. The goal is the sweet spot: sells very well *and* is easy to fulfil.
- **Create Flow, Monetize Flow, Then Add Friction**: Generate demand first, get people saying yes, and only *then* add friction or offer less for the same price. If you can't get demand flowing, you have no idea whether what you have is good.

## Key Concepts
- **High-value one-to-many solutions**: The biggest discrepancy between cost and value. High one-time creation cost, near-zero marginal effort after. "This is exactly why software becomes so valuable."
- **Perpetual assets**: One-to-many deliverables created once that produce high-margin value for years.
- **Overdeliver first, then optimize**: Create cash flow by overdelivering like crazy, then use the cash flow to fix operations. You may not change the offer at all — just build systems that create the same value at lower cost. "Ultimately, this is how businesses beat one another."
- **The step-back method**: If the ideal solution is unaffordable, take one step back at a time until you reach a version whose time/cost you can live with — or massively raise the price so it becomes worth it.

## Mental Models
- **Everyone buys bargains. Some people just buy $100,000 things for $10,000.** That's where you want to live: high prices, but a steal for the value.
- **Think "I get all this for only that."** That sentence in the prospect's head is the design target.
- **Save high-cost delivery for big value-adds only.** If a lower-cost alternative produces the same value, use it.
- **Solutions from one problem seed ideas for others.** Run the cheat codes across every problem, not just the hard ones.

## Anti-patterns
- **Optimizing before you have demand**: "I'd rather do more for every customer and have cash flow coming in, than optimize my business but have zero cash flow and zero idea what to adjust."
- **Under-delivering on your first grand slam offer**: If it's your first, overdeliver like crazy — maybe flying out isn't such a bad idea. Make sales, then make it easier.
- **Trimming by cost alone**: Cutting a low-cost/high-value item because it seems small destroys the stack.

## Worked Example
**Step 4 — one problem, eighteen delivery vehicles.** Problem: *buying healthy food is hard, confusing, and I won't like it.*

*One-on-one:* in-person grocery shopping (take them to the store) · personalized grocery list · full-service shopping (buy their food, 100% DFY) · in-person orientation off-site · text support while shopping · phone call during shopping.
*Small group:* group in-person grocery shopping · teach a group to make weekly lists (once, or every week) · buy and deliver their groceries · off-site group orientation.
*One-to-many:* live virtual grocery tour with live Q&A · recorded grocery tour (shop once, reference forever) · DIY grocery calculator · pre-determined weekly lists shipped with each plan · grocery buddy system (pair customers, costs you nothing) · pre-made Instacart carts for one-click delivery.

**The 100-hour asset.** Before his first gym, Hormozi built a small Excel application: input someone's goals → auto-generate 100+ meals matched to their macros and calories → tell them exactly what to buy, in exact amounts, and how to bulk-prep it. ~100 hours to build. From then on he sold *truly personalized* eating plans at high prices that took **15 minutes each** to produce. Those meal plans have since been used by 4,000+ gyms and hundreds of thousands of people.

**Why the bundle works** — it does three things: (1) solves *all* the perceived problems, not just some; (2) gives **you** the conviction that what you're selling is one of a kind; (3) makes it impossible to compare or confuse your business with the one down the street.

## Key Takeaways
1. Generate delivery vehicles divergently using the six cheat codes before choosing any.
2. Run the 10× / 1⁄10th test to break out of your default solution.
3. Trim high-cost/low-value first, then low-cost/low-value. Keep everything high-value.
4. Prioritize high-value one-to-many assets — high creation cost, near-zero marginal cost, value in perpetuity.
5. Overdeliver first to create cash flow, then engineer efficiency. Not the reverse.
6. Name, describe, and price each bundle. The stack's stated value must dwarf the price.
7. The bundle's job is to make comparison impossible — for the buyer *and* for you.
