# Chapter 5: Pricing — Charge What It's Worth

## Core Idea
Raise price only after you have sufficiently raised value, so the buyer still gets a massive deal — and understand that raising the price **itself increases perceived value**. "Charge as high a price as you can say out loud without cracking a smile."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Price-to-Value Discrepancy**: People buy to get a deal — they believe what they get (value) is worth more than what they give (price). The moment value dips below price, they stop buying.
  - When to use: Every pricing decision.
  - How: Widen the gap. The easy way is lowering price; that is almost always the wrong business decision. Widen it upward instead — sell $100,000 of value for $10,000. Money at a discount.
- **The Virtuous Cycle of Price** (and its vicious inverse):
  - *Lower price →* decreases client emotional investment → decreases perceived value ("it can't be good if it's cheap") → decreases client results → attracts the worst clients, never satisfied until it's free → destroys the margin you needed to hire well, invest in people, and deliver an exceptional experience → your service actually gets worse.
  - *Raise price →* increases emotional investment → increases perceived value → increases client results → attracts the best clients (easiest to satisfy, cheapest to fulfil) → multiplies margin to invest in systems, people, experience, and scale.
  - Why it works: "Those who pay the most pay the most attention." If results require the client to *do* something, investment is a fulfilment input, not just revenue.
- **Premium as a moral position**: If you care about customers, get them as invested as possible — price so it stings a little. That sting focuses attention and adherence.

## Key Concepts
- **Market-efficiency pricing**: Look at market → average competitors → go slightly below → offer a little more for a little less. "The big secret: those competitors they're copying are dead broke."
- **Second-cheapest problem (Dan Kennedy)**: There is no strategic benefit to being the second cheapest in a marketplace, but there is to being the most expensive.
- **Monopoly pricing**: Be so much more expensive that the consumer concludes *"there must be something entirely different going on here"* — that is how you create a category of one and earn monopoly profits.
- **Outworking your self-doubt**: The conviction to charge big-ticket prices comes from delivering the result so many times you *know* this person will succeed.

## Mental Models
- **Price is a lever with asymmetric range.** You can only go down to $0, but you can go infinitely high the other way. Unless you can cut cost to one-tenth of competitors, don't compete on price.
- **Higher price literally increases delivered value.** In a blind wine tasting, participants rated three wines in order of visible price — and it was the *same wine* all three times.
- **"Getting people to buy is not the objective of a business. Making money is."** Not the most customers — the most money.
- **People want to buy expensive things. They just need a reason.** Your job is to supply the reason.

## Anti-patterns
- **Copying competitor pricing**: You are calibrating to broke operators and pricing yourself into market efficiency — just enough at month end to keep going, until the bottom 10–20% wash out and fresh entrants repeat the cycle.
- **Discounting to close**: The one-way road to destruction. It buys a sale and sells the business.
- **Charging premium without the delivery to back it**: "So many wish to shortcut the real work. Do that and you will fail." Conviction must be earned by reps, not asserted.

## Worked Example
**The conversation with his father** — the cleanest premium-price justification in the book. His dad, alarmed that every person in a photo paid $42,000/year, asks if it's legal. Hormozi reframes it as arithmetic:

> "If I made you $239,000 extra this year, would you pay me $42,000?"
> — "For sure. But what would I have to do?"
> "About 15 hours a week."
> — "And how long to make the $239,000?"
> "11 months."
> — "And how much of the $42,000 upfront?"
> "Nothing. Just pay me as you start making the money using the system."

The dad gets it. Note the structure: value stated first and specifically, effort disclosed honestly, time-to-result named, and risk removed via terms — the whole grand slam offer in four sentences.

**The pricing position:** entering a market where low-price competitors charged $500/month and a single high-priced competitor charged $5,000, Gym Launch priced at **$16,000 for a 16-week done-with-you intensive** — 3× the highest player and 32× the low-price players — then upsold 35% into a three-year, $42,000/year agreement. Against an average gym owner take-home of $35,280/year, clients were committing half their annual pay or more.

**The backing data** (voluntary survey, 158 gyms, 11 months in program): topline +$19,932/mo (+$239,000/yr), recurring +$13,339/mo, bottom line from $2,943 → $8,940/mo (3.1×), churn 10.7% → 6.8%, membership prices $129 → $167/mo. The conviction was earned by measurement.

## Key Takeaways
1. Raise value first, then price — the client must still get a lopsided deal.
2. Cheap prices harm clients: less investment, worse results, worst-fit buyers, no margin to serve well.
3. Being the most expensive is a strategic position. Being second-cheapest is not.
4. Price signals quality; the wine study shows perception follows price even when the product is identical.
5. Aim to be so much more expensive that comparison breaks and you become a category of one.
6. Earn conviction through reps and measurement before you ask for a year's salary.
