# Chapter 10: Enhancing the Offer — The Delicate Dance of Desire

## Core Idea
All marketing exists to influence the supply and demand curve. Increase demand and **decrease perceived supply**, and you sell the same product for more money, in higher volume, over a longer time horizon — without changing the product at all.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Five Enhancers** (the whole of Section IV):
  1. **Scarcity** — decrease supply to raise prices and indirectly increase demand through perceived exclusiveness.
  2. **Urgency** — increase demand by lowering the prospect's action threshold.
  3. **Bonuses** — increase demand and perceived exclusivity.
  4. **Guarantees** — increase demand by reversing risk.
  5. **Naming** — restimulate demand and expand awareness among your target audience.
- **"When demand increases, cut supply"** (George's rule): The counterintuitive move. People want what they can't have; people want what other people want; people want things only a select few have access to.
- **Hormozi's Law**: *"The longer you delay the ask, the bigger the ask you can make. The longer the runway, the bigger the plane that can take off."*
- **Fractal 80/20 Demand**: Demand for services is nonlinear. Roughly 1/5th of prospects are willing to pay 5× the price or more.
  - When to use: Deciding whether to sell many cheap units or few expensive ones.
  - How: Skim the top of the pyramid rather than selling the whole pyramid — deliberately leave demand unsatisfied.

## Key Concepts
- **Perceived supply**: What matters is not your actual capacity but what the market believes about it.
- **Pent-up demand**: Unsatisfied prospects whose desire compounds and who buy faster, at higher prices, next time.
- **Desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want** (Naval Ravikant). We only want what we don't have; satisfying desire destroys it.
- **Whisper → tease → shout**: The promotion cadence before a launch.
- **Killing the golden goose**: Satisfying all demand at once leaves you with no idea where the next meal comes from.

## Mental Models
- **Think "ravenous, not merely aroused."** The goal state of your prospect base.
- **Use the supply lever before the price lever.** Cutting units raises price *and* raises future demand. Cutting price does the opposite of both.
- **The person who needs the exchange less always has the upper hand.** Hormozi calls this one of the negotiating and pricing principles that has best served him.
- **Whoever satisfies all demand today has none tomorrow.** Each successive promotion sells fewer units, until eventually you can't make a single sale — the sad state of businesses always chasing more demand for another quick sale.

## Anti-patterns
- **Selling the entire pyramid**: 10 units at $500 satisfies every prospect and leaves zero pent-up demand. The next promotion sells less.
- **Pulling the trigger too early**: Satisfying desire before it has accumulated.
- **Providing zero supply**: The opposite failure — satisfy nothing and you make no money and leave people feeling rejected. (Though "it takes much longer than you think" to reach that failure mode.)

## Worked Example
**The charity auction.** At an Arnold Schwarzenegger After-School All-Stars fundraiser, George — a self-made operator in his late 60s — had convinced the organizer to raise ticket prices from $15,000 to $25,000 *and cut the number of tickets*, in a year of record demand. Result: an extra $1 million raised before the event even started, a more qualified donor pool, and the most successful night in the charity's history — **~$5,400,000 from 100 people, or $54,000 per head.** Each item was auctioned as one-of-a-kind, never available again; Arnold threw in bonuses when two bidders got high enough so the charity captured both donations. Items that wouldn't sell elsewhere for $10,000 sold for $100,000. *The products were unchanged.*

**The two-scenario workshop.** You promote a two-day workshop — whisper, tease, then shout that it launches in a week. Two ways to sell it:

| | Scenario 1 | Scenario 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | 10 units at $500 | 2 one-on-one one-day workshops at $5,000 |
| Revenue | $5,000 | $10,000 |
| Prospects served | All 10 | 2 (80% don't purchase) |
| Cost to deliver | Higher | Lower |
| Aftermath | Zero pent-up demand | 8 people with unsatisfied desire, plus new prospects who see others loving it |
| Next promotion | Sells fewer | Open 3 spots at same price, sell them all, *still* leave demand unmet |

Scenario 2 makes more money, costs less, delivers more value per client, and *increases* demand in the remaining base. The exclusivity itself is the product enhancement.

## Key Takeaways
1. Enhance the offer's presentation, not just its contents — these outside forces are often more powerful than the core offer.
2. Deliberately keep supply and satisfaction below the demand you can generate.
3. Assume fractal 80/20: one in five will pay five times more. Skim the top.
4. Sell out consistently. Pent-up demand compounds across promotions.
5. Delay the ask to grow the ask.
6. Don't fake indifference — build a business where you genuinely don't need any one person's money.
