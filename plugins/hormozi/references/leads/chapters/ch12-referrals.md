# Chapter 12: Lead Getters — Customers (Referrals)

## Core Idea
**The best advertising is a happy customer.** Referrals are the lowest-cost, highest-profit, best-quality leads available — and they're the only lead source that grows **exponentially** rather than linearly.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Referral Growth Equation**: `referrals in − churned customers out`
  - referrals **>** churn → you grow with no other advertising
  - referrals **=** churn → you need other advertising to grow
  - referrals **<** churn → you must advertise just to break even ("a hamster wheel of death")
- **Two Reasons Most Businesses Don't Get Referrals**: (1) the product isn't as good as they think, (2) they don't ask.
- **Goodwill = Value − Price**: The gap between what they pay and what they get. (Economists call it consumer surplus.) Lots of goodwill → word of mouth → referrals. Two ways to build it: lower price or give more value. Lowering price is at best temporary — *"any fool can sell something for less"* (Rory Sutherland). So the question is never how to lower price; it's **how to give more value.**
- **The Six Ways to Get Referrals by Giving More Value** (mapped to the parts of an ad):
  1. **Call out → sell better customers.** Find what your most successful customers have in common, target that, and sell only people meeting the new criteria.
  2. **Dream outcome → set better expectations.** You set the expectations, so you can exceed them. *Slowly lower the promises you make until close rates drop — then stop.* That maximizes both customers and goodwill.
  3. **Perceived likelihood → get more people better results.** Find what the best customers *did*, then make everyone do it.
  4. **Time delay → get faster wins.** A win is any positive experience. Same progress, seven times the wins, if you deliver seven small things at intervals rather than all at once. Updates are wins.
  5. **Effort and sacrifice → keep making your stuff better.**
  6. **CTA → tell them what to buy next.**
- **The Six-Step Best-Results Process** (for #3 above): (1) survey customers to find who got the best results; (2) interview them to find what they did differently; (3) look for the actions they had in common; (4) force new customers to repeat those actions; (5) measure the improvement in average results, speed, and outcome; (6) **match the conditions of your guarantee to the actions that get the best results.**
- **The Seven Ways to Ask for Referrals**:
  1. **One-sided referral benefit** — pay your average CAC to either the referrer or the friend. Ask for a real three-way introduction (call, SMS, or email — not just a name and number), and ask **right when they buy**.
  2. **Two-sided referral benefit** — split your CAC between both parties. *(Dropbox and PayPal's method.)*
  3. **Ask right when they buy** — on the sales contract or checkout page. *"People who do our program with someone else tend to get 3× the results. Who else could you do this program with?"*
  4. **Referrals as a negotiation chip** — *"I can't do less than $500 down, but if you make a three-way text introduction to a few friends right now, I'd be happy to cut the initiation fee."* You can ethically charge differently because you changed the terms.
  5. **Referral events** — points, credits, dollars, or bragging rights within an explicit 1–4 week window. *Bring-a-friend promotion · spouse challenge · accountability buddy · coach challenge.*
  6. **Ongoing referral programs** — talk about the benefits of doing things with others all the time, in content, outreach, and ads.
  7. **Unlockable referral bonuses** — VIP bonuses, courses, tokens, status, training, merchandise, service levels, premium support, extra hours. Works well if you'd rather not pay cash.

## Key Concepts
- **Referrals are worth more and cost less**: Higher LTGP (they buy more expensive things, more times, and tend to pay cash upfront) and lower CAC (a referred customer costs nothing).
- **Not if, but who**: Once someone is a customer, don't ask *if* they know someone — ask **who** they know.
- **Match the incentive to what you sell**: A t-shirt company giving away t-shirts attracts people who want t-shirts. A limited-edition t-shirt for an IT services business may attract nobody who wants IT services. This is also why gift cards work so well.
- **Referring is a risk for the customer**: They're risking goodwill with a friend to gain more of it. They only refer when they believe their friend will very likely have a good experience.
- **"I am compensated tomorrow for the value I provide today."**

## Mental Models
- **Referrals are exponential, the Core Four are linear.** 100 reach-outs → some leads; double it, double the leads. But one customer brings two, two bring four, four bring eight. *"Nothing scales like word of mouth."*
- **Ask yourself: "Why are my customers too embarrassed to tell everyone they know about my product?"** It may be okay — but unremarkable, as in *not worthy of remark*.
- **Asking for referrals only works when you treat it like an offer.** Show the value the customer gets when they refer.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Everyone loves our stuff, we just need to get the word out"**: Said by every small business owner with a product that isn't as good as they think. *"If your product were exceptional, people would already know about it and you'd have more business than you could handle."*
- **Over-promising to close**: Hormozi promised everything including the kitchen sink early on; fulfilling it was a nightmare and left no room to overdeliver.
- **Waiting to ask**: Ask at the moment of purchase, not later.

## Worked Example
**The two weeks nobody noticed.** Leila spotted that Facebook had rejected their ads **two weeks earlier**. Closes had dipped from 21 to 15 per week — but the business was still doing **$500,000 a week from word of mouth alone.**

Months later, on stage in front of 700+ gym owners who had each paid $42,000 to be there, Hormozi paused mid-presentation and asked: *"Who here learned about Gym Launch from another gym owner? Raise your hand."* He regretted it instantly. A few hands. Then more. Then a wave — almost the entire room. Over the life of the business, **$30 million in customers said someone referred them.**

**The referral compounding math:**

| Scenario | LTGP:CAC | Share of LTGP spent to acquire |
|---|---|---|
| No referrals | 4:1 | 25% |
| Every customer brings 2 more | **12:1** | **~8.3%** |

Three customers for the price of one.

**Selling better customers — the PR company.** A portfolio company doing public relations for general small businesses had plenty of sales, heavy churn, and years of no growth. They examined the **lowest-churning** customers and found a shared trait: all in one specific niche, all raising funding from investors. Those customers were only **15% of the business**, so re-targeting felt like risking the other 85% — but the business wasn't growing anyway. They changed the call outs to match the narrow perfect-fit customer. Results: the plateau broke, growth resumed for the first time in years, on track to add millions per month, **advertising costs went down** (more specific messaging → cheaper leads), those customers got more value because the product was built for them, and they **started referring like clockwork.**

**Getting more people better results — Gym Launch.** They tracked customer activities: speed to first paid ad, speed to first sale, attendance on calls. Comparing average customers to the best ones surfaced one finding: **if a gym owner ran paid ads and made a sale within the first 7 days, their LTGP tripled.** Everything reorganized around producing that outcome in week one.

**The referral case studies:** Dropbox gave free storage to both the referrer and the friend — **39× growth in 15 months.** PayPal gave $10 in credit to both sides — a million users within 2 years and 100 million six years later. They still use it.

**The gift card combo** (several strategies stacked): Give every customer a gift card worth **1/3 of their program cost**, transferable to a friend who signs up, expiring in **7–14 days** to force use. The framing shifts from *"Join my program for $2,000 off"* to **"I got this gift card for $2,000 — do you want it? I don't want to waste it."** That gives the referrer status. Combine with the three-way intro: text a picture of the card, and **write the friend's name on it first** — it feels personalized and gives you a legitimate reason to ask for the name.
*Variant: sell the gift cards at 90% off as purchasable gifts only for friends of customers. The referrer looks like they spent a lot, and you get paid to acquire customers.*

**The "run your paid ads for free" effect:** In their service businesses, asking for a referral **right at signup** produced an additional **25–30% of signups as referrals** — 100 clients became 125–130. Operating above 3:1 LTGP:CAC, that referral cash often covered the entire ad spend.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compare referral rate to churn rate. That single equation decides whether you can grow without advertising.
2. If you're not getting referrals, fix the product before the ask.
3. Build goodwill by giving more value, never by lowering price.
4. Narrow your call outs to the customers who succeed most — everything downstream improves.
5. Lower your promises until close rates drop, so you always have room to overdeliver.
6. Find what your best customers did and make it mandatory — then guarantee around it.
7. Ask at the point of purchase, ask for a three-way introduction, and ask *who*, not *if*.
